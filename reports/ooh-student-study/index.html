01 · Executive summary

Five conclusions from the study

Each conclusion is developed and evidenced in the body of the report. Nothing here is repeated as a separate framework later.

Conclusion 1

OOH is an attention and familiarity medium, not a conversion medium

Students do not rely on hoardings when actually choosing an education provider. They rely on reviews, peers and online research.

Conclusion 2

Simplicity decides whether a hoarding is seen at all

Large type, minimal copy, strong contrast and visual communication were consistently preferred. Information-heavy boards get ignored.

Conclusion 3

Frequency appears more valuable than one premium placement

Repeated exposure across student routes may build more familiarity than a single famous location. This should be tested, not assumed.

Conclusion 4

OOH needs a digital continuation to matter

Recognition should lead a student to search SSEI, where content, reviews and student proof complete the persuasion job.

Conclusion 5

Boldness works, but credibility sets the limit

The provocative CA campaign was well received by most CA aspirants asked, yet the discomfort of a minority centred on the Sanjay Saraf name. Bold and witty, not provocative for its own sake.

02 · Research objective

What we wanted to understand

How do students perceive outdoor advertising today, what makes an education hoarding noticeable and memorable, and what role can OOH realistically play in influencing consideration for SSEI?

Noticing

What makes students notice a hoarding, and what makes them ignore one? Which creative formats are easiest to remember?

Recall

Does repeated exposure build familiarity? How important is location? Do students currently recognise SSEI's outdoor advertising?

Decision

Can OOH directly influence institute selection? Which channels create greater recall, and what actually creates trust? How did students respond to the recent provocative CA campaign?

03 · Research approach

How the conversations were conducted

This was intentionally run as an informal exploratory student conversation, not a formal questionnaire that mentioned SSEI upfront. The typical opening was conversational:

Hi guys, do you have five minutes to spare?

Students were told the team was trying to understand opinions about outdoor advertising and that their responses could shape future marketing decisions. Because many were unfamiliar with the term, OOH was first briefly explained: Out-of-Home advertising covers hoardings, billboards, transit advertising and other advertising seen outside the home.

Stage 1 · General OOH perception (SSEI not yet revealed)

  • Whether and where they notice hoardings
  • What attracts attention: colours, creative styles, text quantity, visuals
  • Advertising they found memorable
  • Whether hoardings affect brand perception
  • Whether OOH could influence their choice of an education brand
  • Whom they trust: billboards, reviews, social media, peers

Stage 2 · Brand recall (SSEI revealed)

  • Told the researchers were associated with SSEI
  • Shown selected SSEI outdoor creatives
  • Asked whether they had seen them before
  • Asked what, if anything, they recalled about the brand or creative
  • Asked whether repeated SSEI visibility would affect familiarity or consideration
Why the sequencing matters By discussing OOH before revealing SSEI, the conversation attempted to capture more natural opinions and reduce the risk of prompting students toward the SSEI brand. This was not a scientifically controlled blind study. It was informal, exploratory, directional field research, and the report reads it that way.

06 · Theme 1: Attention

What makes a student notice a hoarding?

Across the interviews (the 70-80% signal above), the same preferences kept recurring: large typography, minimal text, strong contrast, vibrant or solid-colour execution, pictorial communication, and simple relatable ideas. Some students also mentioned 3D elements as attention-worthy where appropriate.

Large type Minimal text Strong contrast Vibrant or solid colour Pictorial Simple & relatable
Core conclusion Students do not want more information from an outdoor creative. They want faster comprehension. As a creative principle derived from these responses (not a measured finding), OOH should be designed for a few-second attention window, not like a brochure.
→

What students notice is only half the equation. Where they encounter the creative determines whether they get enough time to process it.

07 · Theme 2: Context

Where and when OOH gets noticed

Traffic & signals

Idle moments do the work

Students notice outdoor advertising most when stuck, waiting, moving slowly or temporarily idle.

Metro

Large type may perform better

Visibility, waiting time and repeated commuting exposure favour big, readable creatives.

Bus stops

Possibly lower attention

Some students suggested bus stops receive relatively lower deliberate attention. Qualitative observation, not a universal conclusion.

Morning vs return

Return commute may see more

Some students said they are rushed in the morning and notice advertising more while returning. Qualitative observation, not quantified.

Surroundings

Contrast with the environment

A creative that blends into its physical backdrop disappears, regardless of how good the design is in isolation.

Strategic principle Creative should be adapted to location, viewing distance, movement speed and dwell time. One artwork resized for every environment is a compromise in all of them.
→

Being seen once creates attention. Being recognised later requires repetition and distinctive branding.

08 · Theme 3: Memory & recognition

What makes students remember a brand?

Attention creates the opportunity to be seen; recognition requires repetition and distinctive branding. Around 70% of interviewed students (see Data Snapshot) said repeated exposure to multiple smaller hoardings across different places could create familiarity and potentially influence consideration.

For that repetition to accumulate into recognition, students need to identify the brand instantly each time. That argues for consistent SSEI brand assets: distinctive typography, a recurring visual language and a consistent brand device across every placement.

A future opportunity, not a research mandate A smaller but strategically interesting group suggested SSEI could create a distinctive recurring brand asset, such as a mascot or recognisable character. This is a long-term brand-building option to explore and test, not something students overwhelmingly demanded.

09 · Theme 4: Trust & decision

Being remembered is not the same as being chosen

Near-unanimously, students said credible reviews, peer opinions, alumni experiences and word of mouth matter more than billboard claims when actually deciding. Online research, social media and search are where that evidence is gathered.

This is the single most consistent finding in the study, and it defines OOH's realistic role in one journey:

The distinction that matters OOH can contribute to attention and familiarity. It does not appear to be the primary trust or conversion mechanism for most interviewed students.

10 · SSEI brand recall

Are students connecting the creative to the brand?

Stage 2 of the conversations, after SSEI was revealed

When shown SSEI outdoor creatives, the interviews surfaced a brand recognition and recall challenge: creatives could be visually noticeable without being clearly associated with SSEI, and unaided recall of SSEI outdoor advertising was weak in the conversations. Students also indicated that social media contributes more strongly to their recognition of the brand than outdoor advertising currently does.

At the same time, students consistently said that repeated visibility could improve familiarity, which connects this gap directly to the frequency finding in Theme 3: the issue is not that outdoor cannot register, but that today's presence and branding are not registering strongly enough.

FLAG: Recall in this round was captured qualitatively, not counted. No aided or unaided recall percentages exist yet; the measurement framework in section 14 is designed to produce them.
→

This creates an important question for SSEI: how far can the brand push for attention before boldness starts affecting credibility?

An important nuance sat inside the negative reactions. Some students were not against bold advertising itself; their discomfort came from the communication being associated with Sanjay Saraf Sir, whom they regard as a respected professional and educator in finance education.

Strategic insight The lesson is not "controversial advertising works." It is: bold communication can generate strong memorability, but the tone must remain compatible with the credibility and reputation attached to the SSEI and Sanjay Saraf brands.
Memorability ↔ Brand credibility

The territory for SSEI is bold + witty + intelligent, not provocative purely for attention.

The combined findings show that SSEI does not necessarily need more OOH communication. It needs more deliberate OOH communication.

16 · Final takeaway

The research does not suggest that OOH should disappear from SSEI's marketing mix. It suggests its role should become more focused. OOH can create attention, recognition and familiarity; digital content, reviews and student proof then convert that familiarity into trust and consideration. The opportunity is not to say more outdoors, but to become easier to notice, easier to recognise, and easier to validate once a student looks further.
