The question

This was never "do students like billboards?"

SSEI wanted to understand what makes a student look, what gets ignored, what survives in memory, whether the brand is remembered or only the creative, whether location strengthens memory, and whether an outdoor idea can become a conversation that extends into digital behaviour.

How the research was conducted

Five kinds of evidence, one honest label

Field conversations

Student and respondent interviews across multiple fieldwork rounds, opened as neutral conversations about outdoor advertising before SSEI was revealed.

Creative recognition

Respondents were shown relevant creative material, including a masked SSEI creative and a masked competitor creative, and asked about recognition.

Location observations

Feedback on the selected outdoor sites and commute behaviour around them.

Digital reaction evidence

LinkedIn posts and comment threads relating to the "Give a F to your CA Degree" campaign, supplied as screenshots and shown unaltered.

Search-interest evidence

Google Trends material for relevant terms and periods, read as relative interest, never as search volume.

Read this first This is directional research, not a controlled experiment. It reveals patterns, hypotheses and creative directions. It does not prove universal truths about all students in Kolkata, and no claim in this report should be read as one.

What actually stops the eye

Outdoor has a few seconds to earn attention

Across all three fieldwork rounds, respondents kept describing the same interruption mechanisms. The newest 25+ interviews reinforced the first four and introduced a fifth worth testing.

01

Colour

Strong colour fields and bold solid backgrounds interrupt an environment where most boards blend together.

02

Scale

Large type and simple hierarchy survive distance and movement.

03

Simplicity

Nobody reads a brochure placed on a billboard.

04

Distinctiveness

Creative that looks unlike its surroundings gets the glance.

05 · New signal, worth testing

Value

A clearly visible discount or offer may give people an immediate reason to look twice.

Newest interviews · qualitative
Two different interruptions The first four are visual interruption: something looks bold enough to break the environment. The fifth is value interruption: the communication signals an immediate gain for paying attention. The research question is not "should SSEI use discounts?" It is: can a strong value cue earn a second look without making SSEI feel price-led? That belongs in Test Next, not in a blanket recommendation.

Location is not just traffic

When the route becomes part of the memory

The campaign ran on three sites chosen around student movement: AJC Bose Road BQS, Exide Crossing Pillar 21 and Park Street Metro, over two months at a total cost of ₹6,01,800 including GST. The overall response to the site selection was positive; respondents perceived the locations as well considered and relevant to how students actually move.

The most useful observation: among respondents who recognised the creative, roughly half could correctly identify or reasonably guess where they had encountered it. That is not "half of Kolkata remembers our locations." It is something more interesting:

Interpretation When outdoor placement overlaps with a familiar commute, the location itself may become part of the memory structure. Route familiarity, repeated viewing and contextual relevance make the memory easier to store and retrieve.

Seeing is not remembering the brand

Three levels, and the one we could not verify

Recognition of a creative, recognition of its message, and correct attribution to SSEI are three different achievements. This round could only measure the first.

1 · Creative recognition~30% claimed

"I think I have seen this." Claimed recognition of the masked SSEI creative, uncorrected for over-claiming.

2 · Message recognitionNot captured

"I remember what it was communicating." Not separately measured in this round.

3 · Brand attributionNot captured

"I know it was SSEI." The count of respondents correctly naming SSEI or Sanjay Saraf was not recorded.

The masked competitor creative was claimed-recognised far more widely than SSEI's, which says the category can be remembered on these streets; the gap is ours, not the medium's.

The strategic point A billboard that gets remembered without the brand being remembered is only doing part of the job. This is the direct argument for stronger visible SSEI branding on every execution, and for testing Sanjay Saraf as a recognition shortcut.
What specific creatives taught us

Case one: the WhatsApp-style board

What respondents said Recurring pattern

The WhatsApp-style execution received consistently weak feedback from those who evaluated it: insufficient visual dominance, poor suitability for outdoor scale, weak roadside readability, and a communication structure that felt built for a phone screen.

The learning

A device that works in a mobile feed cannot simply be enlarged

Outdoor creative must be redesigned for distance, motion, viewing speed, surrounding clutter and limited processing time. The format is not transferable; the idea might be.

Case two · When the billboard left the street

"Give a F to your CA Degree"

The boards at the Kolkata sites carried one provocative line and a crane hoisting a gold F into the letters CFA. This execution did something most billboards never attempt: it gave people something to react to. This section examines what that reaction does and does not prove.

Among CA aspirants asked about the campaign in the interviews, roughly four in five responded positively: funny, clever, bold, memorable. A smaller minority disliked it, and their discomfort was specific: not boldness itself, but boldness attached to the Sanjay Saraf name, which they associate with professional credibility.

CA-aspirant sub-sample; exact denominator to be added before final publication.

Attention is not agreement A provocative campaign can generate attention without generating agreement. Provocation may be useful when the objective is conversation, but conversation alone is not proof of persuasion. The workable territory remains bold, witty and intelligent, with tone compatible with the credibility of SSEI and Sanjay Saraf.
What the deeper idea might be

The most valuable billboard may not be the one with the most information. It may be the one that gives people something to react to.

Balanced immediately by its condition: reaction is useful only when the audience can connect the conversation back to the brand, and when the attention supports rather than damages the intended positioning.

The LinkedIn evidence in the next section adds a message-clarity lesson: the intended decode, add an F to CA and you get CFA, was not instant. Several commenters had to explain it to the critics, and even a defender pointed out the line reads as an insult "prima facie". A provocative line that needs decoding hands the first interpretation to its critics.

Digital reaction evidence · observed conversation within supplied screenshots

The LinkedIn conversation

The campaign was posted about, photographed and argued over on LinkedIn by people unconnected to SSEI, over a period spanning roughly a month. Six screenshots were supplied and are shown unaltered; click any to enlarge.

Featured: a Chartered Accountant's post (about 4 weeks before the 24 Aug capture) calling the campaign "a direct, offensive, and unprofessional assault on the CA degree", while also decoding the intended wordplay (CA + F = CFA). He photographed the boards himself at a Chowringhee Road junction. Visible engagement on this post: 27 reactions, 5 comments, 1 repost.

Two things make this thread strategically interesting. First, the boards travelled from the street onto LinkedIn through a stranger's phone camera: the amplification mechanism this report hypothesised, caught happening. Second, the argument in the comments did not run one way. The most-reacted comments in the supplied thread defended the campaign's intent (13 and 9 reactions for readings like "they are saying CAs can scale up by pursuing CFA"), while the sharpest criticism attacked the association with Sanjay Saraf's name, and one commenter tagged ICAI's leadership asking for the hoarding's removal.

A separate branding commentator cited the campaign alongside Chai Sutta Bar and WTF as an example of names that "make you stop and look", then asked whether attention converts to long-term trust. The street creative became a third party's case study.

Any movement around the campaign period is a directional correlation, not proof that the billboard caused the search change. The question this evidence answers is narrower and more useful: is there enough signal to justify measuring amplification properly next time?

Each arrow is labelled with what we can actually say about it. None of them means "caused." The earlier research already showed where decisions are made: online, through reviews, peers and content. This pathway is how outdoor attention could feed that machine, and the next campaign should instrument every arrow.

Digital is the continuation of OOH, not its competitor: outdoor interrupts, social explains and discusses, search captures curiosity, the website and YouTube build understanding, reviews and peers build trust, and enquiry follows. A marketing journey hypothesis, not a measured funnel.

The organising framework

Four jobs a billboard can do

The updated research suggests OOH performance involves at least four distinct mechanisms. A creative can score on one and fail another; the report's findings map onto all four.

1

Attention

Can the creative interrupt the environment? Colour, contrast, scale, simplicity, novelty, and possibly value cues.

2

Memory

Can the viewer remember the idea, the message and the placement later?

3

Attribution

Can the viewer connect the memory to SSEI, Sanjay Saraf, and the relevant course?

4

Amplification

Does the idea travel beyond the placement through conversation, sharing and search curiosity?

High attention, low attribution

People remember the line but not SSEI.

High attention, high controversy

People talk about it but do not necessarily like it.

High memory, low action

People remember it but never research the brand.

High amplification, uncertain persuasion

People discuss it online but the brand impact is unclear.

The ideal SSEI execution raises attention + memory + attribution + productive curiosity together, not one metric at the expense of the rest. That framing also keeps the open frequency question honest: the "Give a F" case informs the impact-and-conversation mechanism, but it does not prove one signature board beats distributed frequency. For a comparable budget, that remains the highest-priority experiment.

Recommendations

Act, test, explore

Immediate changes are separated from experiments and long-term brand directions. Nothing untested is presented as proven.

  • Reduce billboard copy to one dominant message
  • Increase typography scale and hierarchy
  • Improve contrast against each site's surroundings
  • Favour visually dominant creative, vibrant or deliberate solid colour
  • Make the SSEI identity unmistakably attributable
  • Never translate mobile layouts directly into outdoor formats
  • Give every OOH campaign a strong digital continuation
  • Prioritise locations relevant to student movement and repeated exposure
  • Design for viewing distance and viewing speed

Discount-led advertising is deliberately absent here. It is a test, not a standing recommendation.

T1

Frequency vs signature impact

Multiple distributed boards vs one high-impact installation on comparable budgets. Measure recall, correct attribution, online conversation, search behaviour and enquiries where measurable.

T2

Value cue vs no value cue

Same visual architecture, with and without a highly visible offer cue. Measure attention and brand perception together: does the offer earn a second look, and does it cheapen the proposition or make price dominate the course?

T3

Bold and witty vs direct and informative

Measure memorability, attribution, credibility, conversation and curiosity. Do not optimise for engagement alone.

T4

SSEI brand vs Sanjay Saraf-led branding

Not "his photo on every board." The hypothesis: could Sanjay Saraf function as a recognition shortcut that helps viewers attribute memorable communication back to SSEI? Test SSEI-only, SSEI plus name, and Saraf-led hooks.

T5

Vibrant vs minimal solid colour

Keep message, location and course comparable; vary only the visual system.

T6

Location-specific creative

Determine whether metro, traffic signal, roadside and bus shelter genuinely require different executions.

T7

Digital amplification tracking

For the next provocative campaign, instrument it: launch time, reposted appearances, social mentions, campaign-phrase and branded search interest, site and direct traffic, QR scans, enquiries. Move from "people said they noticed it" to "we can observe what happened after they noticed it."

  • A recurring SSEI outdoor visual system
  • Recognisable typography and graphic language
  • A signature campaign device
  • Selective use of Sanjay Saraf as a distinctive brand asset
  • An iconic high-impact OOH property, if experiments support it
  • Campaign concepts designed to travel from outdoor into social discussion
  • A consistent OOH to digital landing experience
  • A location-specific creative playbook
  • Mascot or character work, exploratory until recognition testing supports it
Research notes

What this study cannot tell you

Every gap in one place, so the story above stays readable and honest at the same time.

Field survey gaps
  • No exposed vs control breakdown was captured
  • Exact student vs working-professional split unavailable
  • First-mentioned unaided brand not recorded
  • Channel-level exposure split not recorded
  • Card A correct brand-attribution count not recorded
  • Fake-creative Card C was not fielded, so claimed recognition is uncorrected for over-claiming
  • Five-point consideration question not asked
  • Exposure-dose segmentation (days per week passing the site) not captured
Enquiry and operations gaps
  • Exact enquiry counts by exposure not compiled; inbound calls and total leads were observed to be broadly flat through the campaign period, which is reported honestly in the interpretation
  • Formal site operational verification (up, lit, unblocked, full contracted dates) not completed
LinkedIn evidence limitation
The six supplied screenshots (a seventh was a duplicate) illustrate digital discussion but may not represent the entire online conversation. Engagement counts are reported only where clearly legible, and none are aggregated into a reach figure. An impressions figure visible in one author's profile headline is their profile boilerplate, not this campaign's reach, and is deliberately not cited.
Google Trends limitation
Google Trends provides normalised relative search interest, not absolute search volume, and cannot alone establish that the campaign caused any movement. Only related-query exports for West Bengal were supplied; no time series was available, so no campaign-period trend line is drawn or implied.
Newest-interview limitation
The discount and value-cue observation is qualitative and remains so unless respondent-level counts are supplied. Fieldwork rounds are reported separately; a precise cumulative sample is not claimed because full separation of respondent groups is unverified.

Final strategic answer

The research suggests that SSEI's outdoor opportunity is not simply to be present more often. It is to become harder to ignore, easier to remember and easier to attribute. Vibrant, simple and visually dominant communication appears better suited to earning that first moment of attention, and the newest interviews suggest immediate value cues also deserve testing.

The "Give a F to your CA Degree" case introduces another dimension: the strongest outdoor ideas may continue working after the physical exposure ends, by creating discussion and curiosity online. The supplied LinkedIn threads show that happening; the search-interest data stays mixed. Both are directional signals to instrument properly, not proof of causation.

The question is no longer "how many billboards should we buy?"

It is: what makes an SSEI billboard continue working after someone has passed it?
