Why now

The festival we just missed

One of our biggest regrets from the Mumbai trip (27 Sep to 2 Oct) is that we missed Ganeshotsav, Mumbai's biggest festival, by just a few days. The city had only just finished its immersions when we landed. A festival that size is a whole economy on the street, and we were there to see only its last traces.

We have said that this year SSEI wants to focus on putting out good, quality content. The festival season is the one time of year when culture, spending, credit and gold all peak together, and every city has a money story waiting to be told. We feel there is no better time for us to execute this than now.

We will not miss the next one. Mahalaya is on 10 Oct, and the season runs straight through to New Year's Eve.

The idea

One festival, three kinds of money

Every Indian festival is a small, complete economy. A pandal has fundraising, a budget, sponsors, suppliers, contract labour and a hard deadline. A garba night has ticket pricing and inventory risk. We show people the money moving underneath something they already love, with the culture first and the finance lesson landing as the "aha".

School of Finance

A campaign for the School of Finance

We think this trip would work very, very well for promoting the School of Finance. It shows the kind of teaching we want to be known for, in the field rather than on a slide.

01

Practical thinking

Every film turns a festival into a working case: a budget, a supply chain, a pricing decision. That is the School of Finance approach of learning finance by applying it.

02

On the ground

We go to Kumartuli's workshops, Ahmedabad's garba grounds and Varanasi's ghats, and learn from the people who run these economies. Finance is understood where it happens.

03

From the root level

We start from the clay, the cloth and the diya, and build up to GDP. Students see how the big numbers are made from small decisions.

04

All of India, together

Kolkata, Ahmedabad, Ayodhya, Varanasi, Shillong and Goa, plus Chhath from Bihar, in one season: the School of Finance as the whole of India coming together in one classroom.

How it carries the brand: each long film and series closes with a School of Finance line, the Festival Economist challenge invites students into it, and the season's films become ready-made campaign material for admissions.

Calendar

Our shoot days, in two phases

Each bar is the days we are on the ground, never more than three in a city we travel to. Phase one covers Mahalaya, Durga Puja and Navratri in October; phase two covers Diwali, Chhath and Dev Deepawali in November, and Christmas and New Year in December.

Shouldn't Dussehra be in Ayodhya?

Ayodhya's signature celebration is Deepotsav, on the eve of Diwali (7 Nov), which marks Ram's return home. That is the bigger spectacle and the better money story, so Ayodhya sits in phase two on Diwali. Dussehra itself (20 Oct) falls inside Durga Puja week, and Bengal celebrates it as Bijoya Dashami (21 Oct), so phase one captures it in Kolkata.

Why Ahmedabad for Navratri

Ahmedabad is garba's cultural home. It has free, neighbourhood-run pol garba in its UNESCO-listed old city, the biggest ticketed grounds, and the state's own Vibrant Navratri, so one city gives us heritage, business and household stories together. Surat's strongest story is its textile trade, which runs all year and can be a shoot of its own.

Major festivals only

We dropped smaller regional fairs and kept the festivals with national weight. Guru Nanak Jayanti falls on the same day as Dev Deepawali (24 Nov), so we can cover only one; Varanasi is the stronger visual and money story. Hornbill in Nagaland stays open for discussion.
